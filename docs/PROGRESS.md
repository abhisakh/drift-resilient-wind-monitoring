### Step 3: the operating curve

Why: the existing fault-injection test raises an alarm on any single sample
above a 1 % threshold, so a model that alarms more often "finds" a ramp
sooner by chance. The curve adds a persistence requirement (a dispatch needs a
sustained alarm), a 0 K placebo ramp (the chance level), and a comparison at
EQUAL false call-outs per turbine-year.

Written: `src/windmill/operating_curve.py`,
`scripts/pipeline/16_operating_curve.py`, `tests/test_operating_curve.py`, a
generated README section, and a `curve` Makefile target.

Result so far (deterministic arms, 14.5 turbine-years, real ENGIE data):

1. A single-sample rule is dominated by chance: the deployed model "detects" a
   0 K placebo ramp in most windows. The earlier headline detection figure
   (90 %, about 1.5 days) was measured that way.
2. At equal false call-outs per year, an arm that knows nothing about the
   operating point (a constant median, recalibrated monthly) catches +2 K and
   +4 K ramps about as well as, or better than, the deployed model. For +8 K
   ramps all arms catch about 98 % and the conditioned arms are somewhat
   faster.
3. On a synthetic turbine where load really drives temperature, the same code
   shows the conditioned model winning clearly, so (2) is a property of the
   ENGIE data and not of the harness.

This cuts against the claim that conditioning on the operating point buys
early warning at equal false call-outs, and it undercuts the dashboard's
"naive versus conditioned" cost view, whose naive arm is not calibrated the
same way.

Three checks were run before believing it, all on the real data, and all left
the finding standing:

* the best setting of each arm chosen on half the windows and scored on the
  other half (the optimism of picking the best of a grid removed);
* a fault whose heat scales with load instead of a flat offset (the constant
  arm does better still);
* the age of the reference: false dispatches rise by roughly 1.4 to 2 times
  for a one-year-old reference, for every arm, not just the conditioned ones.
  That is far below the 46.5x headline, which lets each arm keep its own
  threshold rule.

Still open: the ramp is a simulated offset on one channel, and the shape of a
real fault is unknown.

With the learned arm in (complete run, all four arms):

* flat offset: LightGBM is the best conditioned arm (at the standing dial it
  catches more of the +4 K ramps than the deployed model, with fewer false
  dispatches and the same chance level), and it leads at a tight budget;
* load-proportional fault: LightGBM is the WEAKEST arm and the constant median
  the strongest, so the ranking flips with the fault's shape;
* reference age: LightGBM is the most sensitive to an old reference.

So no arm is robustly best, and the earlier statement that the learned arm
"detects better" is true only for one fault shape.

### Step 4: the real anomaly, on the same dial

The simulation used a persistent step. R80711's anomaly is the one real fault
we have, so the dial was run on it (`operating_curve.lead_time_on_anomaly`,
stored as `r80711_lead_time` by `make curve`). Nothing is injected: each arm is
fitted on the month before 2017-08-15 and scored on R80711 up to 2017-10-15; a
dispatch before the onset (2017-09-01, when R80711 first ran hotter than its
siblings) counts as a false alarm.

Two corrections to earlier work came out of it:

* **"The 80 C crossing" is not an event.** R80711 had 223 samples above 80 C in
  2017 BEFORE September, on July 12, 19, 20, 23 and more. The reference event is
  now the first sustained hot spell: the first day at or after the onset with at
  least 30 samples above 80 C. That is 2017-09-08. My first version measured
  against 2017-08-15 and reported nonsense (a dispatch 28 days "early"); that was
  caught before anything was committed.
* **The anomaly is not a persistent step.** It runs about two weeks (Sep 5 to
  18), with spikes of +13 to +17 K in bursts, then it stops; there is a second
  episode in early October. The excess grows with load: about 2 to 3 K at low
  and middle loads, 6 K at 60 to 80 %, 14 K at 80 to 100 %. So it resembles the
  load-proportional shape, in spells.

Result for the deterministic arms (the learned arm needs the author's
`make curve`):

* at the standing 1 %, one-hour setting the first dispatch came about 4.6 days
  AFTER the first sustained hot spell began, with no false dispatch before the
  onset;
* at every arm's most profitable setting (for either fault shape) it NEVER
  dispatched during the anomaly;
* NO setting of the dial, for any of the three arms (0 of 40 each), warned
  before the hot spell without also raising a dispatch before the onset.

So the one real case does not support "detects defects early" for these arms,
and it contradicts the economic optimum drawn from the flat-offset simulation:
a rule that waits for a long unbroken alarm cannot fire on a fault that comes in
spells. The cost ranking has to be read with that in mind, and the fault
simulation should gain an intermittent, load-proportional shape.

Caveats: it is one machine, once, so there is no detection rate and no chance
level. The reference was fitted on a calm month with almost no high-load
operation, so the top load bin is partly extrapolation (its mean excess is
large even in the quiet stretch, from a handful of samples). The "30 samples" in
the hot-spell definition is a judgement.

Written: the function, the script integration, a generated README section, the
headline card (it shows the real-anomaly result when the curve carries it and
falls back to the old single-sample figure otherwise), scorecard readings, and
tests (pass in the sandbox; the two dashboard tests were updated and not run).
The committed `results/operating_curve.json` does NOT yet carry
`r80711_lead_time`; the dashboard shows the fallback card until `make curve` is
re-run. To finish, in this order: `make curve`, `make cost`, `make report`,
`make test`, `make dashboard`.

#### The learned arm, and what else was tried

The author's `make curve` added LightGBM, and it behaves exactly like the
others on the real anomaly: standing 1 %, one-hour setting 4.9 days after the
hot spell began; never dispatched at its most profitable settings; 0 of 40
settings early and clean. So the limit is not the model.

Two more ideas were tried in the sandbox, as throwaway experiments that are NOT
in the repo (so they are exploratory, not reproducible from it):

* a peer-corrected residual (R80711's residual minus the median sibling
  residual at the same timestamp), which should cancel the effects the
  turbines share: 0 of 40 settings early and clean;
* detectors that accumulate evidence (a rolling mean over 6 h to 7 days) on
  both the own-model and the peer-corrected residual: 0 of 16 each.

#### Why: observability

Between the onset (1 Sep) and the first hot spell (8 Sep) R80711 had NO
samples at load of 60 % or more: a calm week. The excess lives at high load
(about +4 to +13 K above 60 %, against 0 to 3 K at low load, inside about 3.5 K
of noise), and the first high-load day, 8 Sep, is the first hot spell. The
training month held 96 samples at 60 % or more, 2.7 % of it. So for this fault
the warning time is bounded by when the turbine first works hard, not by the
model or the dial. That is now recorded in the artefact
(`high_load_coverage`) and generated into the README.

What it implies for the project, not yet built: a monitor should report how
much of the high-load regime it has seen (a coverage indicator) and treat "no
high-load operation yet" as "not yet observable", not as "healthy". It also
means the claim to detect faults EARLY is not supported by the one real case:
the supportable claim is that it detects this fault once the load that reveals
it occurs.

### Step 7: the historical confirmation (the project as the author defines it)

After a run of side experiments, the author restated the project: every month a
fresh reference is fitted on the previous month's healthy data only; it learns
how load, weather and season move each sensor; an alarm is raised when the
sensor behaves worse than that healthy expectation, not merely above a fixed
number; and because the data are historical, the next month is already known,
so the prediction can be confirmed. That is what this step shows, with nothing
but the dashboard's own monthly engine (`live.monthly_cross_check`).

Built: `src/windmill/confirmation.py` (the per-month facts and the aggregate),
`scripts/pipeline/18_monthly_confirmation.py` and `make confirm` (every month of
every turbine; `--merge` assembles per-turbine runs), a generated README
section, a dashboard view (four cards, a chart of the monthly error, a table;
no new widgets), and `tests/test_confirmation.py`.

The committed `results/monthly_confirmation.json` was produced by the real
script, run per turbine and merged (it needs no LightGBM); `make confirm`
regenerates it.

Result (232 turbine-months, 228 assumed healthy, alarm setting z above 3,
sustained for an hour, fixed limit 80 C):

* **The month is predicted.** Median error 1.81 K (90th percentile 2.35 K);
  88 % of months within 1 K of unbiased; 1.73 to 1.92 K for every turbine.
* **False alarms in healthy months.** The model raised 7.7 dispatches per
  turbine-year, a plain 80 C limit 2.4. On the three siblings the limit raised
  none (they never reach 80 C) and the model 4.2 to 5.4 a year. On R80711 the
  limit raised 45 (9.6 a year) and the model stayed quiet on 24 of them: load and
  weather explained that temperature, which is the effect the author described.
  But the model raised 78 of its own there (16.7 a year).
* **The real anomaly months.** The model dispatched in 3 of the 4 months, first
  on 13 Sep, 16 Oct and 23 Nov; the 80 C limit in all 4, first on 8 Sep, 5 Oct
  and 2 Nov, so earlier each time, and 31 dispatches against 3.

So the prediction claim is confirmed; the claim that the model raises fewer
false alarms than a fixed limit holds only for the stretches where the fixed
limit is crossed by a healthy hot machine (R80711), and at this default setting
the model's own false alarms outnumber the limit's. The setting is the open
question: the operating curve found settings with under one false call-out a
year, which this confirmation has not yet been run at.

Caveats: health is assumed, not labelled; one real anomaly; the later anomaly
months are partly fitted on the anomaly (the monthly reference's documented
blind spot); the alarm setting is the dashboard default, not tuned.

#### Cross-check against a second implementation of the deterministic model

The author asked for a cross-check with the deterministic model. The monthly
engine IS the deterministic model (a median per load bin plus an ambient
offset), so the useful check was a second, independent implementation of the
same model class: the backtest harness's `DeterministicArm`, fitted on the same
30 days before each month and scored on the month (no trimming). Run in the
sandbox as a throwaway script that is not in the repo.

* the alarm threshold used was z = 3 (the dashboard's default; the engine
  function's own default is 4), stored in the artefact;
* across 228 healthy turbine-months the median error was 1.81 K (engine) against
  1.90 K (harness); per-turbine medians within 0.1 K; 83 % of months within
  0.25 K of each other; correlation of the monthly errors 0.76;
* the harness has a smaller bias (median -0.07 K against the engine's -0.38 K).

So the 1.8 K figure is not an artefact of the engine.

One month stands out: November 2015, on all four turbines at once, where the
engine's error was 1.6 to 2.2 K and the untrimmed harness's was 3.7 to 4.3 K.
The engine trimmed 10 to 13 % of that training month, a typical share (the
median over all months is 11 %), so the trimming amount does not explain it; the
rows it removed were evidently influential. That is consistent with the
author's rule of training only on healthy data, but the cause of the odd rows in
October 2015 has NOT been traced (a site-wide data issue is a guess, not a
finding). Separately, the engine trims a lot in some months (up to 42 % in
December 2015), which is worth understanding.

#### Turning the alarm down: what suppressing false alarms costs

The author's view: a model that predicts well should be able to suppress false
alarms. So the history was re-scored at stricter alarm settings (z above 3, 4,
5, 6, 8; held an hour or six hours), plus a plain 80 C limit held for the same
times. Nothing is refitted: each month already stores every row's z-score.
Built into `confirmation.summarise_month` (`settings`, `fixed_settings`), the
aggregate, the README and the dashboard; the artefact was regenerated, and
`tests/test_confirmation.py` checks the grid by hand.

Result (false dispatches per turbine-year in healthy months, fleet; R80711
anomaly months alarmed of 4):

* z above 3, an hour: 7.7, 3 of 4 (first Sep dispatch 13 Sep);
* z above 4, an hour: 3.7, 1 of 4;
* z above 5 to 8, an hour: 2.3 down to 0.9, 0 of 4;
* any z held six hours: 0.4 down to 0.1, 0 of 4;
* plain 80 C limit: 2.4 and 4 of 4 (first Sep dispatch 8 Sep) held an hour; 0.8
  and 3 of 4 held six hours.

So false alarms CAN be suppressed with any model, simply by raising the threshold
or the hold time, and the model gets well under the fixed limit (0.9 against 2.4
at z above 8). But every setting that does so alarms on none of the four anomaly
months, while the fixed limit alarms on three or four. The only model settings
that alarm on the anomaly (z above 3 and, once, above 4) have more false alarms
than the fixed limit. On R80711 itself, the only turbine with an anomaly, the
fixed limit at an hour had 9.6 false dispatches a year and 4 of 4 months alarmed;
the model at z above 4 had 8.4 and 1 of 4.

The reason is in the numbers already here. A monthly error of 1.8 K is an
average over the month. An alarm is decided row by row against a threshold that
has to clear the tail of the residual (6.6 K at z above 3 in the dashboard),
while the real anomaly's excess is 2 to 3 K at low and middle loads and only
reaches 6 to 14 K at high load. Prediction accuracy therefore lowers the noise
but does not by itself make a small deviation alarmable.

#### Is the weather really ERA5, and is it right for that place and time?

The author asked to be sure that weather and temperature come from the online
Copernicus ERA5 data via Open-Meteo. Checked, in throwaway sandbox scripts that
are NOT in the repo.

Provenance, confirmed: `acquire.py` requests `archive-api.open-meteo.com/v1/era5`
(the ERA5-only endpoint, not the blended "best match"), at 48.4569 N, 5.5847 E,
hourly `temperature_2m`, `wind_speed_100m`, `wind_direction_100m`,
`surface_pressure`, 2012-12-01 to 2018-02-01, UTC, m/s. The stored file is the
nearest ERA5 grid cell (48.471 N, 5.597 E, 439 m), has all 45,336 hours with no
gaps, a verified checksum, and a plausible climate (January 2.1 C, July 19.0 C).

Which model inputs come from ERA5: the 2 m temperature, in every bearing
temperature model (the engine, the backtest, the curve, the confirmation); the
turbine has no ambient sensor. Wind is mixed: the deterministic temperature
model uses no wind at all (it uses the measured power as the operating point),
while the LightGBM arm and the wind ablation use the turbine's OWN anemometer
(`Ws_avg`), not ERA5. That departs from the author's rule.

Accuracy of ERA5 wind at the site, against the turbine's anemometer, hourly:
correlation 0.79 to 0.81 (about 0.85 to 0.87 in winter, 0.69 to 0.78 in
summer), rmse 1.5 to 1.7 m/s, mean 6.4 m/s against 6.0 to 6.35.

A WRONG TURN, recorded: the wind lined up best with ERA5 shifted by 1 h in
winter and 2 h in summer, which looked like a clock error of the DST size, and
reading the raw stamps as UTC did improve the wind correlation and a power-curve
fit (168.8 to 154.5 kW). That hypothesis is WRONG. The raw files show the wall
clock skipping 02:00 to 03:00 at the spring switch exactly as the offset label
flips, which only a genuine local-time clock does, and ERA5's own 2 m temperature
peaks at 14:00 UTC in both seasons, as it should. The summer lag is ERA5's wind:
its 100 m wind peaks at 01:00 UTC at night while the turbine's peaks at
20:00 to 21:00. So the loader's time handling stands; the "correction" only
compensated for ERA5's wind phase. Nothing in the repo was changed for this.

The useful finding: as an INPUT to the deterministic bearing-temperature model,
ERA5 wind helps, where the turbine's own wind barely does (weekly backtest, 4
turbines, median error):

* load + ambient 2.102 K;
* plus the turbine's wind 2.091 K (-0.012 K);
* plus ERA5 100 m wind 2.017 K (-0.070 K), better in 79 % of weeks, and on every
  turbine (-0.056 to -0.081 K), level with LightGBM (2.019 K).

Small (about 4 %), consistent, and about ten times the gain from the ambient
temperature (+0.007 K, which is unchanged by any clock treatment). NOT yet
built into the repo, because it would change every number downstream: adding
ERA5 wind to the deterministic model and the monthly engine, and replacing
`Ws_avg` with it in LightGBM, then re-running the backtest, the curve, the cost
model, the confirmation and the report.

#### "We use all the sensors, so why can't it stop the false alarms?"

The author suspected a big mistake. There was a gap: every reference was fitted
on three things only (the channel, power, ERA5 ambient), although the turbine
has ten temperature sensors. Tested in throwaway sandbox scripts (not in the
repo), on the confirmation's months, linear load-bin models:

* Adding the other sensors makes the PREDICTION much better: the noise of the
  bearing-2 residual falls from 2.75 K (load + ERA5) to 1.03 K (+ gearbox oil
  and inlet), 1.10 K (+ gearbox bearing 1) and 0.60 K (+ all nine).
* But false alarms in healthy months do NOT fall (at a 1 h hold they rise, to
  7 to 16 a year with all nine), and the R80711 anomaly is no longer detected
  at all (0 of 4 months) once any gearbox neighbour is an input.

Why, measured on R80711 against each sensor's own load + ERA5 model: in the
hot-spell week the WHOLE gearbox ran hot (bearing 2 +4.1 K, bearing 1 +3.9 K,
oil sump +4.7 K, inlet +4.3 K) while the generator and nacelle ran cooler. A
model that predicts bearing 2 from its neighbours therefore predicts the fault
as normal: masking.

Requiring the four gearbox sensors to agree (their average, or all four above
their own threshold) did not help either: false alarms 2.3 to 2.7 a year at the
strictest setting and 1 of 4 anomaly months, the same as bearing 2 alone.

What this says about the goal:

* better prediction does not by itself mean fewer false alarms, because the
  model's alarm threshold is set relative to its own noise, so the number of
  its alarms is set by the alarm rate chosen, not by its accuracy;
* there are few false alarms to stop in this data: the plain 80 C limit raised
  2.7 a year across the fleet, ALL of them on R80711 (the siblings never reach
  80 C);
* where the limit does raise false alarms (R80711, 11.1 a year at a 1 h hold),
  the load + ERA5 model suppresses most of them (2.7 to 4.5 a year at its
  strictest settings), at the cost of later detection of the real anomaly.

Not yet measured: a two-stage rule in which the 80 C limit triggers and the
model vetoes the alarms that load and weather explain. The confirmation already
shows the model stayed quiet on 24 of the limit's 45 healthy-month dispatches
on R80711; what such a rule does to the anomaly months is not measured.

#### The author's idea: compare with the healthy siblings at the same moment

The author pointed out that on the same day the siblings feel the same weather,
so R80711's heating can be compared with theirs directly. Two corrections came
out of checking how this had been done so far:

* **The "onset" of 1 September is an artefact.** C.7 in `15_backtest.py` scans
  for sibling divergence only from 2017-09-01 (`days = pd.date_range(lo, hi)`), so
  1 September is simply the first day it looked at. Every "before the onset"
  figure above inherits that date.
* **That check did not compare at the same moment:** `_peer_median_at_wind` pools
  the siblings' temperatures by wind-speed bin over 1 July to 15 October, so
  July heat and October cool are mixed.

Tested properly (throwaway script, not in the repo): at the same ten-minute
timestamp and similar power (within 10 % of rated of the siblings' median), the
gap between a turbine's bearing temperature and its siblings' median; the daily
median gap; and its change from the previous 30 days' median gap (last month's
normal). Healthy days: sd 1.19 K, 99th percentile 3.94 K.

* R80711's change from normal before the hot spell: +3.6 K on 15 Aug, +3.8 K on
  18 Aug, +2.2 on 30 Aug, +2.5 on 6 Sep; then +5.7 on 8 Sep, +5.8 on 11 Sep,
  +7.1 on 13 Sep. So this signal does rise before the hot spell, at low and
  middle loads, where the own-model residual did not.
* But R80711 crosses +3 K on 8 to 25 healthy days EVERY year since 2013
  (2013: 25, 2014: 8, 2015: 14, 2016: 12), against 0 to 4 for each sibling. On
  R80711 a mid-August flag is not distinguishable from its usual behaviour.
* A rising monthly count before the fault (2017: Jun 2, Jul 3, Aug 3 days) is
  no larger than late 2016 (Oct 2, Nov 3, Dec 3), which was not followed by a
  known fault.
* December 2017, assumed healthy because the anomaly span ends on 30 November,
  had 11 flagged days of 31: the problem probably did not end in November, so
  that assumption is likely wrong.

So the same-moment sibling comparison is the most promising early signal found:
quiet on the healthy siblings (about one day a year at +3 K) and rising on the
faulty turbine before the hot spell. It is not yet a validated early warning,
because the one turbine with a fault is also the one that is noisy against its
siblings all along, and there is only one fault.

Smoothing (the author's suggestion), same throwaway script: a rolling median of
the daily change over the past N days. Healthy-day noise falls from 1.16 K (one
day) to 0.90, 0.68 and 0.58 K (3, 5, 7 days). Flagged days per turbine-year
above +2 K: on the siblings 5.2, 1.8, 0.8, 0.5; on R80711's healthy days (through
2017-07, December 2017 now excluded) 34, 19, 12, 8.7. R80711's own flags survive
smoothing because they come in multi-day episodes, not single noisy days. The
first flag from August 2017 is early August (before the anomaly) at +2 K, and
12 or 13 September at +3 K with a 3- to 7-day median, so later than the limit.

### Step 8: the daily check (the author's design)

Built: `src/windmill/daily_check.py`, `scripts/pipeline/19_daily_check.py`
(`make daily`, about 4 minutes, per-turbine runs and `--merge`),
`results/daily_check.json`, a README section, a dashboard table, and 14 tests in
`tests/test_daily_check.py` on a synthetic turbine with known answers.

The policy: each month the model is trained on at least a month of healthy
readings (no standstill, no 12 h warm-up after a restart, no flagged day,
nothing before the last maintenance) and frozen for the month; every day the
day's mean of actual minus predicted is compared with a tolerance; a run of hot
days is one call-out at the end of its first day; a confirmed fault keeps the
model frozen until maintenance; after maintenance the model is blank until a
new month of healthy data exists. Stood in for, as there are no records:
maintenance = a restart after 3 days or more without running; the inspection =
the documented anomaly span (from 15 August 2017).

Results (false call-outs a year; R80711 first call-out on the fault):

| tolerance | healthy siblings | R80711 | first on the fault | fault days hot |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 K | 1.5 | 8.8 | 3 Oct | 9 of 84 |
| 1.5 K | 0.1 | 6.4 | 15 Aug | 58 of 111 |
| 2 K | 0.1 | 4.8 | 15 Aug | 48 of 115 |
| 3 K | 0.1 | 0.7 | 13 Sep | 4 of 115 |
| 80 C limit | 0.0 | 2.9 | 15 Aug 11:10 | |

* The daily noise on healthy days is a standard deviation of 1.0 to 1.2 K, so
  1 K is inside the noise. At 1 K the many flagged days starve the training and
  the model is BLANK through August and September 2017, which is why it is the
  latest to catch the fault.
* At 1.5 to 2 K the model catches the fault on its first day and, frozen, keeps
  flagging it through January (unlike the monthly engine, which re-learned it).
* R80711 ran hot against its own healthy reference from May 2017 (2 K: May 2,
  June 6, July 9 hot days), against at most 4 call-outs a year in 2013 to 2016.
  These count as false alarms here; they may be the fault's early stage. Unproven
  without labels.
* Cold days are seasonal (siblings: 12 to 17 % of checked days in winter, 2 to
  5 % in summer), so a weather effect the model does not capture in winter, not
  sensor faults; they are recorded, not dispatched.
* Coverage: 60 to 73 % of running days are checked; 174 to 366 running days per
  turbine are blank (start of record, after maintenance).

#### Does wind direction distort the sibling comparison?

The author's concern. Throwaway check, not in the repo: each turbine's
bearing-2 gap to its siblings' median (same moment, similar power), healthy
period, by ERA5 100 m wind direction (8 sectors). The turbines stand in a rough
north-south line about 400 m apart, so N and S winds put them in each other's
wake.

* Direction does shift the gap: by 0.5 K (R80721) to 1.3 to 1.6 K across
  sectors, about the size of the healthy daily noise (1.2 K) and well below the
  fault (4 to 7 K).
* The largest shifts are with EAST and SOUTH-EAST winds, not N or S: R80711 runs
  +1.5 and +1.95 K above its siblings in E and SE winds against +0.5 to +0.9 K
  otherwise; R80736 runs 1.5 K below in SE winds. E and SE winds are 14 % of the
  time. So it is not simply wake; the cause is not established.
* This may explain part of R80711's history of running hot against its siblings.

Implication: the sibling comparison's "normal gap" should be learned per wind
direction sector (from the same training window), or E/SE days treated with a
wider tolerance. The per-turbine, per-sensor models are not compared across
turbines, so this does not enter them directly.

#### Step 10: CARE Farm A against EDP's own records (alarm log and failure logbook)

Goal: a real reference for "false alarm". ENGIE has no alarm or failure
records; CARE Farm A comes from EDP's open data, and EDP publishes its turbine
event log (alarms) and a logbook of confirmed failures.

Data added: EDP onshore wind farm, Portugal, 2016-2017 (Mendeley Data,
doi 10.17632/zjxjnjp3xs.2, CC BY 4.0, 174 MB), in `data/raw/edp/` (zip,
sha256 c65e2dc7...adb1) and `data/raw/edp_extracted/`. It holds SCADA for T01,
T06, T07, T11 (not T09), the on-site met mast, the failure logbook (28 rows)
and the event log (256,401 rows). Not yet in `MANIFEST.json`. All the work
below was done with throwaway scripts, kept (not committed) in
`data/external/care_farm_a_scratch/`; nothing is in the pipeline yet.

**Integrity of CARE Farm A.**
* The 22 event files are copies of one record per turbine: where they overlap
  (1.4 million timestamp pairs) every reading is identical.
* Each CARE turbine is one EDP turbine, reading for reading (gearbox
  temperature, ambient, wind and power identical on 99.98-100 % of 38,000 to
  48,000 shared timestamps): CARE 11 = T01, 21 = T06, 0 = T07, 13 = T11;
  10 = T09 (through the logbook only). CARE power is power / 2,050 kW.
* CARE's anonymisation moved each file by a fixed number of days so that it
  starts on the same calendar date in 2022: 2,191 days, or 2,192 for files 40
  and 71, which start before the leap day of 29 February 2016. That is the
  one-day slip found first; nothing was altered.
* The five turbines see the same weather at the same moment (daily anomalies
  r = 0.99), share the same farm-wide data gaps, and follow their power curves
  (0.25-1.8 % of readings off the curve). Ambient and component temperatures
  are recorded in whole degrees.
* EDP's met mast (independent, on site) agrees with each turbine's ambient
  sensor at r = 0.99 (turbines 0.7-1.2 K warmer) and wind at r = 0.92-0.96.
* CARE Farm A's status column is NOT an operational record: every "downtime"
  block is exactly 14 days ending at a logbook failure (26 of 28 failures;
  the other two lie outside the data), every "service" block exactly 3 days,
  and the turbine produced power during 35-84 % of "downtime". Power, not
  status, is used to find stops.

**How a fault is labelled "real".** EDP's logbook is 28 manual records of
confirmed component failures (a repair or replacement). CARE's anomaly events
are windows ending at a logbook failure (all 12 within 10 minutes; events 51
and 72 are the same T06 gearbox failure). Nobody judged the turbine alarms;
overheating that tripped a turbine without a repair is not in the logbook. In
this work an alarm is "real" when a logged failure of the same component
follows within 30 days (my rule, not EDP's or CARE's).

**The turbines' own alarms (T01, T06, T07, T11).** Temperature and hydraulic
warnings grouped into alarm spells (consecutive alarm days): 56 spells, 16 real
and 40 false (71 %) by the 30-day rule; they warned before 10 of the 21 logged
failures on these turbines.

**Weather source.** The model uses the met mast's measured temperature, and the
turbine's power. Swapping in the turbine's own ambient sensor changes the daily
error by under 0.1 K (T07: gearbox 0.70 K, generator stator 2.63 K, transformer
4.39 K, hydraulic oil 3.53 K); dropping weather makes it worse (gearbox
0.98 K, stator 3.93 K). Weather is not the bottleneck; the transformer and the
generator are driven by cooling fans the model does not know about.

**Our model as a veto on those alarms.**
* As built: a verdict on only 19 of 56 spells. The model was blank on most
  alarm days: 18 after a logged repair of the component (new healthy month),
  4 with no healthy month left after flagged days (the anti-poisoning rule),
  7 at the start of the record, 6 days with too little running, 2 with no data.
* With two coverage fixes (a provisional model on two weeks of healthy data
  after a repair, and a sibling comparison while there is no model): a verdict
  on 50 of 56, but the veto suppressed real alarms as often as false ones
  (3 K: 20 of 40 false and 8 of 16 real; 5 K: 26 of 40 and 9 of 16). Several
  "real" failures are not heat (the T01 gearbox oil pump, a transformer fan,
  oil leaks), so a normal temperature says nothing about them.
* With the rule "never suppress a real alarm" (temperature alarms only, both
  references normal for 95 % of the day): nothing is suppressed, false or real.

**Temperature only.** Temperature alarms ("Hot HV trafo", "Hot generator",
"High temperature Gen", "High temp. Gen bearing", "High gear temp") and
failures where heat is the cause or the symptom (7 on these turbines). 41
alarms, 9 followed by a temperature failure of the component within 30 days.

On Farm A's 41 temperature alarms, the model says:

| Model's verdict | Alarms |
|---|---|
| Real excess heat: peaked more than 5 K above the healthy prediction | 34 |
| Explained by load and weather | 5 |
| No verdict (no healthy reference that day) | 2 |

* The alarms are short, genuine heat peaks: the hottest readings ran a median
  14 K (real) and 16 K (false) above the healthy prediction, while the daily
  mean was only 6 K and 4 K above. Real and "false" peaks overlap almost
  completely, so temperature cannot say which will end in a failure. Below
  3 K, 3 false alarms and no real one are explained by load and weather; one
  real alarm (T07 transformer, a week before its high-temperature failure)
  peaked only 3 K above healthy.
* Every "Hot HV trafo" (19,845) and "Hot generator" entry records 0 kW, the
  generator ones at 145-204 C: protective trips. While windy (5 m/s or more)
  the turbines stood still 3.1 h a day during "false" alarm spells, 1.1 h a
  day in the 30 days after, against 0.5 h a day normally (alarms followed by a
  failure: 4.2 and 2.2 h a day). The "false" alarms cost production and were
  followed by more stopping. Some stops may have other causes (curtailment).
* Case: T01's transformer had 13 alarms in summer 2016 (9 real heat by the
  model), all "false" by the 30-day rule; the logbook records "Transformer fan
  damaged" on T01 in August 2017. T07's transformer: 14 alarms, all real heat
  by the model; 6 followed by a high-temperature failure within 30 days, 8
  within 90.

**Reading.** The model behaves as designed: trained on healthy data with the
weather, it confirms real excess heat. Most of the turbines' "false"
temperature alarms on Farm A were real overheating that tripped the turbine;
the logbook does not list them because no part was repaired. The model cannot
judge faults that are not heat (pumps, fans, leaks, pressure): it has no such
signals here. Framing for the project: alarm triage (real excess heat:
inspect; explained by load and weather: set aside), not "false alarm
suppression" on Farm A. On ENGIE, where the fixed 80 C limit fires on hot
high-load days, the model does suppress weather-made alarms.

**Which data have non-thermal signals.** ENGIE and CARE Farm A: none (Farm A
has fan, pump and pressure only as text events in EDP's log). Farm B:
drivetrain and tower vibration; its 6 faults are transformer-cell heat (3) and
rotor or main bearing damage (3). Farm C: 238 sensors including hydraulic
pressures, pump and fan motor currents, flow rates, oil levels, vibration;
its 27 faults are largely non-thermal (pitch, gear-oil pump coupling, rotor
brake and hydraulics, oil leak, cooling valve, transformer overpressure).
Farm C is therefore treated separately: what the same method detects when
those signals are present.

Code change: `daily_check.simulate` takes `maintenance_extra`, recorded
repairs that start the model over exactly as a long stop does (one new test).

**Saved as a pipeline step** (`make care-a`, `scripts/pipeline/20_care_farm_a.py`
-> `results/care_farm_a.json`; code in `src/windmill/edp.py` and

#### Step 11: the same triage on ENGIE's fixed-limit alarms

`make engie-triage` (`scripts/pipeline/21_engie_triage.py` ->
`results/engie_triage.json`). ENGIE publishes no alarm log, so the alarms are

* Only R80711 ever hit the limit: 27 spells. 26 real excess heat, 1 explained
  by load (November 2016), 0 by weather, 0 without a verdict.
* 19 of the 26 lie in the documented fault period; the other 7 are June to
  early August 2017, the months before it (the candidate early warning seen
  in the daily check).
* At the hottest readings of the real-heat spells: typical 70 C, load
  +9.4 K, weather -0.2 K, excess +3.6 K. The siblings see the excess too
  (95th percentile 6-9 K above their normal gap).

**What this changes.** On both farms the fixed-limit alarms that fired were
mostly real excess heat, and the weather explained almost none of them (Farm
A +1.5 K, ENGIE -0.2 K). The claim "fixed limits fire because of the weather"
is not supported by these data. What the data support: a fixed limit cannot
say whether the heat is load or something wrong; the model can, and it
confirms the real excess; on ENGIE it also flags fault days below the limit
(48 of 115 checked fault days in the daily check). The pitch deck's slide on
why alarms happen was worded around hot afternoons and must say this
instead.

## Open questions

* What should the project claim now? The honest candidates are: keep the
  reference fresh (a roughly two-fold effect), use a persistence rule, and
  accept that a recalibrated adaptive limit is a strong baseline that the
  conditioned models have to beat. Step 5 should decide the alert design with
  that in mind.
* The R80711 lead time (C.7) still uses the first day with any alarmed sample,
  which is the single-sample rule the curve shows to be mostly chance. It
  should be redone on the same dial (Step 4).
* Does a load-proportional ramp (heat that scales with load) change the
  ranking between arms? Answered: no, it favours the constant arm further.
* "False dispatch" in the dashboard's monthly loop is an alarm episode with no
  row above 80 C inside it; the curve counts every dispatch on a healthy
  window instead. The two definitions should be reconciled.

#### Step 12: the daily check on CARE Farms B and C

`scripts/pipeline/22_care_farms_bc.py` → `results/care_farms_bc.json` (`make care-bc`).

Every CARE dataset of Farms B (15) and C (58) runs the same monthly policy as
on ENGIE (healthy model per sensor, at least a month of healthy data, frozen for
the month, never trained on flagged days), with the site's own outside-air
sensor in place of ERA5 (the farms stay anonymised). Sensors are grouped into
components; outside air, hub and cabinet sensors are left out.

**A fixed 2 K tolerance does not carry over.** Farm B's gearbox bearings are
held at 64–67 °C by their oil cooling and miss their healthy prediction by about
0.4 K a day, like ENGIE's. Its transformers (core near 107 °C), generator and
rotor bearings and pitch motors lag their load by hours and miss by 3–5 K on
ordinary days. At 2 K, healthy Farm B turbines got about 50 call-outs per
component-year. So the tolerance is now learned too: each component is judged
as one signal (the mean miss of its sensors), against median + 4 robust SDs of
its own last 90 healthy days, never below 2 K. Only past days are used.

| Healthy datasets, call-outs per component-year | Monthly model | Frozen model |
|---|---|---|
| Farm B (9 datasets, 5 components each) | 2.98 | 2.73 |
| Farm C (31 datasets, ~7 components each) | 2.91 | 4.26 |

The monthly refresh cuts false call-outs by about a third on Farm C and not at
all on Farm B. With a learned tolerance the frozen model partly adapts too (its
tolerance follows the median), so this is a smaller gap than ENGIE's
26.37 % vs 0.57 % of readings, which compared fixed tolerances.

**Faults caught on their own component:**
- Farm B, 0 of 6. The three "high temperature in transformer cell" events got
  hotter with load (e.g. event 7: 64 → 71 °C as power rose 0.19 → 0.28 of
  rated); the healthy model explains that heat as load, so no excess and no
  call-out. The three rotor/main-bearing damages show no heat signature (one
  turbine was standing still, so nothing was checked).
- Farm C, 2 of 27 (both hydraulics: events 47 and 28). 8 faults have no
  temperature sensor on their component (communication, wiring, converter
  fuse, yaw grease pump, own-consumption measurement); of the other 19, 11
  fault windows were never checked: the turbine stood still around the fault,
  the model treated the stop as maintenance and was waiting for a new healthy
  month. Most of Farm C's faults are pitch faults (electronics, batteries,
  encoders), which temperature does not see.

**What this means for the claim.** The method's benefit is clearest where a
component's temperature is regulated or fast (ENGIE and Farm B gearbox
bearings). Across Farms B and C a temperature-only check catches few of the
labelled faults; the farms' faults are mostly mechanical, electrical or pitch
faults. Coverage after standstills is the main technical gap: a stop of 3 days
or more resets the model for a month, so a fault that stops the turbine is
seldom checked. Next: a shorter restart path (re-use the last model after a
stop that was not maintenance), and vibration/pressure signals for Farm C.

**Maintenance only when it is logged (follow-up).** On ENGIE a stop of 3 days
or more stands in for maintenance because no log exists. Farm C logs service
(CARE status 3), so it now also runs with the rule: a stop of 3 days or more is
maintenance only if service was logged during it; any other stop keeps the
model (after the 12-hour warm-up). Short service visits are not maintenance:
Farm C logs 22 service periods of 6 h or more per turbine-year (tried first as
the maintenance rule, it reset the model so often that checked days fell from
23,472 to 4,364). Farm B logs no service status, so it keeps the stand-in rule.

| Farm C | Stand-in rule | Logged-maintenance rule |
|---|---|---|
| Healthy: false call-outs per component-year | 2.91 | 2.95 |
| Healthy: checked component-days | 23,472 | 24,393 |
| Faults caught on own component | 2 of 19 | 2 of 19 |
| Fault windows never checked | 11 | 11 |

One fault gained coverage (event 67, transformer overpressure: 1 → 30 of 59
days checked, no call-out). The 11 unchecked windows each have a stop of 3 days
or more with service logged at or just before the fault, very likely the repair
visit itself; the model rightly waits for a new healthy month after it. Farm C's
headline now uses the logged rule.

**Thermal memory and per-sensor judging (follow-up).**

*Thermal memory* (scratch test on Farm B, not in the pipeline): the healthy
model was given a "remembered load", an exponentially weighted average of power
with a half-life of 0 (as built), 1, 2, 4 or 8 hours. Median robust SD of the
daily miss on healthy datasets:

| Component | 0 h | 1 h | 2 h | 4 h | 8 h |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| gearbox | 0.30 | 0.30 | 0.36 | 0.38 | 0.39 |
| generator | 2.12 | 2.31 | 2.46 | 2.96 | 3.24 |
| transformer | 3.61 | 3.06 | **2.84** | 3.38 | 4.63 |
| rotor bearing | 3.24 | 2.82 | 2.82 | 2.69 | **2.22** |

A modest gain for the transformer (−21 % at 2 h) and rotor bearing (−31 % at
8 h), a loss for the generator, none needed for the gearbox. No Farm B fault
was caught by memory alone. Not adopted.

*Averaging hid the transformer faults.* The three Farm B faults are "high
temperature in transformer **cell**". On the cell sensor alone they stand out
(event 19: +12.2 K mean in the window, +0.6 K before; event 7: +5.3 K vs
−2.2 K; event 34: +2.6 K vs +1.4 K), while the seven winding sensors beside it
stayed normal; the component average hid them. The earlier "explained by load"
reading of these faults was wrong. Each sensor is now judged against its own
learned tolerance (median + 4 robust SDs of its last 90 healthy days, never
below 2 K); a component is hot when any of its sensors is.

| | Farm B, averaged | Farm B, per sensor | Farm C, averaged | Farm C, per sensor |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Faults caught on own component | 0 of 6 | **2 of 6** | 2 of 19 | 2 of 19 |
| False call-outs per component-year (healthy) | 2.98 | 6.66 | 2.95 | 6.24 |
| Frozen model, same rule | 2.73 | 6.97 | 4.26 | 7.23 |

Caught on Farm B: event 19 (transformer cell) and event 53 (rotor bearing 2
damage), each on single hot days; requiring two days in a row would lose both.
The extra false call-outs come from a few noisy sensors: Farm B's generator
(26.9 per year: generator bearing 1 jumps 10–15 K month to month on some
turbines; the slip ring follows nacelle air), Farm C's pitch (14.8) and
hydraulics (9.5). Gearbox (1.4–1.5), rotor bearing (0.3–0.9) and Farm B pitch
(0.6) stay low. Open decision: per-sensor judging is the physically right rule
(a fault shows on one sensor), but it needs noisy sensors handled, e.g.
flagged as data quality, before the false call-out rate is acceptable.

#### Step 13: the conclusion, and why a trusted alarm is worth acting on

`scripts/pipeline/23_alarm_value.py` → `results/alarm_value.json` (`make alarm-value`).

* **The weather term, on EDP.** The daily check on the four logged turbines,
  with the met mast's outside temperature and with a constant in its place.
  Without the weather the healthy prediction's daily error grows by 16 %
  (transformer), 51 % (generator) and 43 % (gearbox), median over turbines.
  On ENGIE's oil-cooled gearbox bearing the weather adds almost nothing; the
  README states both.
* **Lead time and the cost of waiting.** For each logged failure: did the
  triage confirm real excess heat on the same component in the 60 days before,
  and how long did the turbine stand still in wind (≥ 5 m/s) from 2 days before
  to 14 days after. T06 "Generator replaced" (2016-10-27): confirmed 54 days
  before, 113.2 h (normally 7.7 h). T07 transformer cooling repaired
  (2016-08-23): 51 days before, 91.8 h (normally 11.2 h). Not every failure was
  warned: T07's generator bearings (2017-08-20) cost 136.8 h with no confirmed
  alarm.
* **The README now has a generated Conclusion** (between CONCLUSION markers,
  every figure from results/, held to the same test as the other generated
  sections): what it is, what the results support, its scope, and where it
  sits beside commercial monitoring and Vervlimmeren et al. (2025).
  New generated results sections: the EDP alarm value and CARE Farms B and C.
* **Wording to keep consistent elsewhere.** The R80711 anomaly span is set by
  this project from its siblings; the ENGIE release has no fault label, and the

#### Step 17: a frozen model judged with last year's same month

* Why the limit caught days the model missed on R80711: after the fault was
  confirmed the model stayed frozen on its May to July 2017 training, and
  judged September 2017 to January 2018 with it. In summer the turbine rarely
  runs at high power (95% of the training readings were below 56% power), so
  the model had no healthy reference for the windy winter hours, when the
  bearing is hottest: from November it could judge 6,092 readings,
  last year's models 7,209.
* The rule (windmill.daily_check, `last_year_when_frozen`, on in the ENGIE
  daily check): in a frozen month, judge the turbine with its own healthy
  model for the same month a year earlier, if last year's model for the
  frozen model's month still predicts the frozen model's healthy readings to
  within 1 K (a repair that changed the component fails this).
* Result (results/frozen_fallback.json, `make frozen-fallback`): limit-only
  days 6 → 1; R80711 hot days
  43 → 57 of 103; the three healthy turbines judged
  the same way over the same months: 0 → 0 false hot days in
  271 days.
* Tried and not adopted: a seasonal correction from the healthy siblings
  (caught 1 of the 6 days) and the past window with the most similar weather
  (5 of 6, but its picks were 2 to 3 years old and added false hot days on
  the healthy turbines).
* CARE Farms B and C keep the plain frozen model (the switch is off by
  default there), so their published numbers are unchanged.
